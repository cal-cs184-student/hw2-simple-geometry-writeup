Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier Curves with 1D de Casteljau Subdivision

Bezier curves are smooth parametric polynomial curves that are defined by a series of control points. These control points determine the Bezier curve such that it always passes through the first and last control points, and the intermediate points between act as "magnets," pulling on the curve and helping shape it despite not actually lying on the curve. Bezier curves play a major role in computer graphics because they enable us to model approximations of more complex shapes found in the real world.

The de Casteljau algorithm is a simple way to, given the control points for a Bezier curve, find points that lie on the Bezier curve itself at specific parameter values. For n control points and a parameter value of t, we can calculate n-1 intermediate control points at t, where an intermediate point is calculated as: \(p_{i} = (1 - t)*p_{i} + t*p_{i+1}\), for \(1 \leq i \leq n-1 \). Once a subdivision of intermediate points is computed, we can then recursively continue computing these sets of intermediate points until a single point is interpolated, and this point will lie on the Bezier curve. A full Bezier curve can thus be evaluated from these control points by completing all recursive subdivisions at all values of the parameter t. In my code, I implemented this process so that each call to the evaluation function completes one level of subdivision by interpolating between the adjacent pairs of given points and returning these interpolations; if there is only one given point, no interpolation needs to occur and the process ends there.

Part 2: Bezier Surfaces with Separable 1D de Casteljau

The concept of Bezier curves can be generalized to 3D, where a Bezier surface is defined by a grid of control points in 3D space and parameters u and v. We can think of each row of points as a set of 2D control points, and then use the de Casteljau algorithm and the parameter u as above to recursively subdivide until we reach a final point for every row in the grid. This resulting set of intermediate points then acts as control points for a Bezier curve parametrized by v in the column-wise direction, so we can once again use the de Casteljau algorithm with the parameter v to obtain the final point, which lies on the Bezier surface at the parameters u and v. Therefore we are separating the evaluation of the Bezier surface into evaluation of Bezier curves along one orientation, then evaluating the resulting curve along the other orientation. Additionally, evaluating the Bezier surface first by columns and then by rows produces the same result as first by rows and then by columns.

Implementation of this process was simple: for each row vector in the grid of control points, I used the evaluate1D function (which returns the final result point on the Bezier curve from a given set of control points) with parameter u, appending the results into another vector. I then used the evaluate1D function on this result vector with parameter v to obtain the point which lies on the Bezier surface. Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-Weighted Vertex Normals

Triangle meshes are an alternative to Bezier curves when it comes to representing surfaces. However, while triangle meshes are easier to directly render than Bezier curves, they do require more memory and also don't represent smooth surfaces very well. This latter part becomes especially evident when attempting to shade surfaces, where flat shading (shading every triangle according to its face's normal vector) results in a very blocky appearance. Phong shading creates a drastically improved appearance, providing smooth shading and realistic specular lighting despite not changing the triangle mesh. This approach is accomplished by interpolating vertex normals across the triangle, where the normal of a vertex is calculated as the normalization of the sum of all area-weighted normals of the faces connected to the vertex.

I implemented this in my code by iterating through all of the half-edges that emerge from the given vertex. For each half-edge, I calculate the area of the associated triangle face as \( \frac{1}{2} * ||\vec{u} \times \vec{v}|| \), where u and v are the vectors that extend from the current vertex to the two other vertices of the triangle. I multiply this face area by the face's normal, then add it to the sum of the face normals over all of the faces connected to the given vertex. Finally, I normalize the sum of these face normals, which produces the area-weighted vertex normal that can be used for Phong shading. Part 6: Loop Subdivision for Mesh Upsampling

Mesh upsampling is when we convert a coarse mesh into a finer one with more triangles, effectively creating a higher-resolution mesh for a better appearance or modeling granularity. As a global operation that works on the entire mesh, this is a method that requires many combinations of edge splits and flips. The loop subdivision algorithm upsamples a mesh by dividing each triangle of the mesh into 4 smaller ones, then updating all vertices of the subdivided mesh according to weighted combinations of their surrounding vertices.

When implementing this algorithm, I followed the advice given in the spec. First, I computed the updated positions of all existing vertices with the given formula \( (1 - (n * u)) * original\_position + u * neighbor\_position\_sum \), then computed the positions of the new vertices that would be created by subdivision with the formula \( \frac{3}{8} * (A + B) + \frac{1}{8} * (C + D) \), where A, B, C, and D are the vertices of the 2 adjacent triangles. I then performed the actual subdivision process, splitting all original edges of the mesh and then flipping the new edges that are connected to 1 new vertex and 1 old vertex. Finally, I updated all vertices in the mesh with their new positions, as given by the formulas above. The following screenshots demonstrate certain shapes after iterations of loop subdivision.

Loop subdivision tends to significantly round out sharp corners and edges. This is especially apparent for the torus mesh, where just 1 round of subdivision transforms it from a more hexagonal, angled ring to a rounded loop shape. It is possible to mitigate this rounding effect by pre-splitting edges near (but not on) the sharp edges of the shape; I split some edges on the top and right sides of the torus in the second row of screenshots, and this preserved the flared edges of the outer side of the torus even after upsampling the mesh.

Similar deformations of sharp edges occur with the cube mesh, although the cube also ends up looking strangely lopsided as though a skew transformation has been applied. This is because of the way that the updated position for a vertex is calculated: since it's a weighted sum of the vertex's original position and the sum of its neighboring vertices' positions, a vertex that has more neighbors will therefore be "pulled" towards them more, compared to a vertex with fewer neighbors. Some of the cubes' vertices have 4 neighbors while others have 5, and the vertices with 5 neighbors are pulled more by these neighbors and towards the center of the cube, creating the lopsided mesh. In the second row of images, I simply split the edges bisecting each face of the cube, which created vertices of equal degrees (6) at all corners of the cube and restored symmetry to the cube mesh even through various levels of subdivision.
